# SIWES Technical Report: Reference Documents

Department of Computer Science, Lagos State University of Science and Technology (LASUSTECH)

This file collects the three reference documents used to define how a Log2Def technical report should be structured, formatted and written. Place it at `docs/reference/REFERENCE_DOCUMENTS.md`.

---

## 0. How to use this file

### 0.1 The three documents and their authority

| ID | Document | Authority | Use it for |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | SIWES Coordinator's guidelines (text notice) | **Highest, for structure** | Headings, order and numbering of the report. Also states what the report is assessed on. |
| B | Signed 2026 report by an ICT Center trainee (photographs of printed pages) | **Highest, for layout and quality benchmark** | Cover, certification, page numbering, heading style, week-block writing, tone, level of specificity, length per chapter. An accepted, signed report. |
| C | 2025 senior's report (typed text) | **Content-style example only** | How to describe projects in a software internship and how to link the experience to the course of study. Older ITF-style structure; not an authority for format. |

### 0.2 Conventions in this file

- **Anonymisation.** Real student names, matriculation numbers, supervisor names and signatures are replaced with placeholders such as `[STUDENT NAME]`, `[MATRIC NO]`, `[DIRECTOR NAME]`, `[SUPERVISOR NAME]`, `[SIGNATURE]`. Do not add real personal data to the repository.
- **Transcription.** Document B was transcribed from photographs. Words that were unclear are marked `[?]`. Text hidden by page show-through was not transcribed. Pages not photographed are listed in section 2.1.
- **Verbatim text** is shown in `text` code blocks. Typing errors in the originals are preserved unless a note says otherwise.
- **Analysis** (observations, implications) is the author's reading of the documents and is marked as such. Observations about fonts, spacing and sizes are estimates from photographs.

### 0.3 Out-of-scope items mentioned in the sources

The coordinator's notice lists items needed for the defence beyond the technical report: employer's evaluation, students' logbooks, ITF Form 8 and a departmental seminar (oral presentation). These are outside Log2Def's current scope.

### 2.4 Content and style analysis (analysis)

**Voice and grammar**
- First person throughout the student's own work: "I was taught", "My supervisor taught me", "I installed", "I worked on", "I also learned", "I used".
- Passive voice only for general statements ("Safety precautions were also emphasised").
- Past tense for activities. Sentences are mostly 15 to 35 words. Paragraphs have two to four sentences.
- British spelling in most places ("organisation", "programme", "emphasised", "fulfilment", "centre"), with slips (see 2.5).

**Specificity (the main quality marker)**
- Named components and tools: motherboard, CPU, RAM, SSD, PSU, CMOS battery, Rufus, Avast, Malwarebytes, Smadav, Windows 10 and 11, Windows Defender Firewall, Safe Mode.
- Quantities: "about 30 systems", "12 new HP desktop computers", "for the Computer Science Laboratory".
- Procedures compressed into one sentence (RAM installation steps joined with commas).
- Causes of faults listed in one sentence (dusty RAM, faulty VGA connections, defective power supply units, failing hard drives, blocked cooling fans).

**Structure of work description (3.1)**
- A one-sentence chapter introduction, then week-range blocks: heading "WEEK 1–2: Title", then one short paragraph. Four blocks cover twelve weeks. Each block is about 70 to 100 words.

**Chapter Two**
- A three-paragraph history and description: what the organisation is and its role, where it is located, what it is responsible for, who heads it and which staff categories it has. Then an organogram of the head and unit heads. About 160 words of prose.

**Chapter Four**
- 4.1 Summary (about 115 words): names the areas of work, then one sentence on bridging theory and practice.
- 4.2 Conclusion (about 80 words): value of SIWES in general, a statement that the field extends beyond programming, confidence gained in specific tasks, readiness for employment.
- 4.3 Recommendations (about 75 words): three numbered items, each addressed to a named party (ITF and NUC; university management; the host unit) and each specific to the student's experience (modern equipment, workbenches, project participation).

**Dedication and acknowledgement**
- One sentence each, plain, naming God, parents, the director, the supervisor and unit staff.

### 2.5 Limitations of this sample (analysis)

- Its headings differ from the coordinator's list: Chapter One has three sections (1.1 Brief Information about SIWES, 1.2 Objectives of SIWES, 1.3 Scope / Period Spent), Chapter Two is titled "Organisation" with two sections, and Chapter Three is titled "Work Done, Skills, Equipment, Challenges and Pictures". Chapter Four matches. Either the department tolerates variation or the guideline was issued after this report. **Follow the coordinator's list.**
- Spelling slips: "program" in the dedication (against "programme" elsewhere), "ICT Center" (kept as the organisation's proper name), "STUDENT" singular in the scheme's name on the cover and in the text, a likely typo on the cover, and "Center" alongside "centre".
- Section headings appear in a different typeface from the body.
- The host organisation is the university's own ICT Center, so Chapter Two draws on institutional knowledge that most students will not have.

### 2.6 Implications for the generator (analysis)

1. Use the cover wording and order above, with the university logo added at the top.
2. Cover page numbered "i", certification "ii" (confirm with the coordinator).
3. Week-range blocks with descriptive titles, in prose, with named tools, systems and recorded quantities.
4. Summary, conclusion and recommendations of about 75 to 120 words each, specific to the student's work, with the recommendations addressed to named parties.
5. Calibrate length: about 280 to 300 words per page, not more.
6. Dedication and acknowledgement stay very short and are written only from what the student provides.

### 3.4 What to learn and what to avoid (analysis)

**Learn from it**
- Tell each major project as a short story: what it is called, who uses it, what problem it solves, what the student built, and what technologies were involved.
- State the student's role and the department posted to in the opening paragraph.
- Include a paragraph that links the experience to named courses of study, since the coordinator says the report assesses this linkage.
- Mention how the work changed over time ("in the later weeks").

**Avoid**
- Bullet lists of tasks (it uses them; the signed 2026 report uses prose).
- Unsupported metrics ("reducing manual workload by 80%", "improved the workflow to 90%", "client acquisition to 50%"). Numbers like these must come from the student, never from the AI.
- Informal or faulty wording: "loose" for "lose", "manually have to handle", "send out receipt", the project name repeated before each description ("Vitse Marketing Pipeline - Vitse Marketing Pipeline is built for..."), run-on sentences.
- Claims of achievements outside the training (hackathon wins) mixed into the report.
- American spelling mixed with British ("program", "practiced", "maximize", "fulfillment").
- Missing certification, contents and chapter structure, since the coordinator's list now requires them.

## 5. Conventions derived from the references (analysis)

Each convention lists its evidence.

1. **Use the coordinator's headings verbatim and in order** (A).
2. **Write to the assessment aim:** show understanding of the industrial process and link the work to the course of study (A statement of purpose; B 4.2; C significance paragraph).
3. **First person, past tense, formal but plain English** (B, C).
4. **Week-range blocks with descriptive titles, in prose** (B 3.1).
5. **Named tools, systems, features and recorded quantities** (B 3.1; C project stories).
6. **Tell each major project as a short story** (C), but in prose, not bullets (B).
7. **Summary, conclusion and recommendations of roughly 75 to 120 words each, specific to the student's work; recommendations addressed to named parties** (B 4.1 to 4.3).
8. **Very short dedication and acknowledgement, written from what the student supplies** (B).
